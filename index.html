03 Course description

Logic and Critical Thinking is an inquiry that focuses on the examination of arguments as its primary subject of investigation. This course introduces students to the fundamental concepts of logic and the methods of logical reasoning. Its primary objective is to equip students with essential skills for analyzing, evaluating, and constructing arguments.

By honing these skills, students will enhance their ability to apply critical thinking in their thinking processes and written expression. By actively engaging with the material, students will cultivate intellectual curiosity and refine their ability to think critically, empowering them with invaluable skills that will serve them well in their academic endeavors and beyond.

04 Course objectives

By the conclusion of the course, students will have achieved the following learning outcomes:

  • Comprehend the interconnection between logic and philosophy: grasp the intricate relationship between logic and the broader field of philosophy, and understand how logic serves as a foundational tool for philosophical inquiry.
  • Identify key branches of philosophy: become familiar with the central areas of philosophy, including metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, and aesthetics, and recognize their significance.
  • Recognize the importance of studying logic and philosophy: gain an appreciation for how these disciplines contribute to critical thinking, intellectual growth, and a deeper understanding of complex concepts.
  • Understand fundamental logical concepts and arguments: acquire a solid understanding of basic logical concepts, such as premises, conclusions, validity, and soundness, and be able to distinguish between deductive and inductive reasoning.
  • Identify fallacies in communication: develop the ability to identify common errors in reasoning and effectively critique flawed arguments in spoken and written communication.
  • Cultivate linguistic precision: develop a heightened sensitivity to the importance of clear and accurate language use, refining skills in constructing well-formed arguments.
  • Construct sound arguments and evaluate arguments: develop the necessary skills to construct sound arguments and evaluate the strength and cogency of arguments presented by others.

07 Course contents

Chapter One: Logic and Philosophy

  • Meaning and Definition of Philosophy
  • Core Branches of Philosophy
  • Importance of Learning Logic and Philosophy

Chapter Two: Basic Concepts of Logic

  • Basic Concepts of Logic (Premises, Conclusions, etc.)
  • Techniques of Recognizing Arguments
  • Types of Arguments: Deductive and Inductive
  • Evaluation of Arguments: Validity, Soundness, Strength, Cogency

Chapter Three: Logic and Language

  • Cognitive and Emotive Meaning of Words
  • Intensional and Extensional Meaning of Terms
  • Types, Purposes, and Techniques of Definition
  • Criteria for Lexical Definitions

Chapter Four: Basic Concepts of Critical Thinking

  • Meaning and Definition of Critical Thinking
  • Principles and Standards of Good Argumentation
  • Factors Affecting Critical Thinking
  • Relevance of Critical Thinking

Chapter Five: Logical Reasoning and Fallacies

  • Formal and Informal Fallacies
  • Categories of Informal Fallacies: Relevance, Weak Induction, Presumption, Ambiguity, Grammatical Analogy

Chapter Six: Categorical Propositions

  • Components and Attributes (Quality, Quantity, Distribution)
  • Venn Diagrams and the Square of Oppositions
  • Evaluating Immediate Inferences
  • Logical Operations: Conversion, Obversion, and Contraposition

10 Course policies

Attendance

Students are expected to attend every class. Absences hinder your progress and prevent participation. Attendance is marked as follows:

  • 5/5 — arrives on time and actively participates.
  • 4/5 — arrives on time but does not participate.
  • 3/5 — arrives late but participates, or provides a valid excuse for absence.
  • 2/5 — arrives late and does not participate.
  • 0/5 — absent without a valid excuse.

Communications

Official channels for this course are e-mail, Canvas messaging, and in-class announcements. It is your responsibility to check these regularly. When emailing the instructor or any professor, use a formal tone appropriate for a professional environment.

Assignment submission and formatting

All assignments must be submitted on the day they are due. Late submissions will not be accepted; an extension may be considered only if the student provides reasonable and tangible evidence for failing to submit on time. All submitted material must be typed or electronically composed (e.g., MS Office, Google Docs) and submitted as a PDF — handwritten work will not be accepted. Proofread your work for spelling, grammar, and clarity before submission.

Class discussion and participation

Active student participation is essential for this course. The classroom aims to be an environment where all students feel free to express their thoughts, ask questions, and challenge ideas without fear of sounding unsophisticated. The best learning happens in a relaxed and open environment.

11 Academic integrity & AI usage

Academic honesty. The university's policies on academic honesty, as described in the AASTU Senate Legislation of 2017, are upheld and vigorously enforced. All work submitted must be your own. You are free to use reference material, but you must cite all sources properly. Copying any part of another's work and presenting it as your own is plagiarism, and any violation will result in immediate dismissal from the class with a failing grade. Allowing someone to copy your work is treated the same as copying someone else's.

AI usage. Generative AI tools may be used to assist with your work, under two core principles. Accountability: you're 100% responsible for the work you submit, including fact-checking, editing, and critically evaluating any AI-generated output — submitting factually incorrect or poorly reasoned work is your failure, not the AI's. Transparency: you must cite any use of generative AI. At the end of any assignment where you used an AI tool, include an "AI Usage Statement" naming the tool, how it was used, and any key prompts used; failure to do so is a violation of the Academic Honesty policy.
